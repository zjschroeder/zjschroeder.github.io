Moral and Partisan Differences in Cancel Culture Discourse

NLP

8.6 million tweets, partisan lean inferred from bios, moral and emotional content scored three ways. The sides frame the same cancellation in different moral vocabularies, and outrage builds in the days after attention peaks.
Published

July 22, 2026

Modified

September 21, 2026

In brief. Opinions about cancel culture are everywhere online; data on it are scarce. I collected 8.6 million tweets about cancellations, inferred each author’s partisan lean from their bio, and scored every tweet for moral and emotional content with dictionaries and transformer classifiers. The result is the largest behavioral dataset on cancellation talk I have found, and it answers three questions any organization that listens to the public would ask: can you tell who is in a conversation without surveying them, how differently do the sides frame the same event, and how does the tone change as a story unfolds?

Why this matters

“Cancel culture” gets invoked in newsrooms, legislatures, and product-policy meetings with total confidence and very little data. Underneath the punditry sit two questions that come up whenever an organization listens to the public: who is talking, and how are different groups framing the same thing? They are the same questions for a canceled celebrity, a product recall, or a nonprofit’s campaign. This project treats cancellation as a test case for answering them at scale.

Data

Note

8,572,185 original English-language tweets from 4,035,488 users, collected through the Twitter Academic Research API in late 2022, deduplicated, retweets removed. Platform terms prohibit redistributing tweet or bio text, so no tweet, bio, or account is quoted or identified and every number here is an aggregate; the public figures who were the subjects of cancellations are named. The figures are computed live from small aggregate CSVs in the site repository; analysis code is at github.com/zjschroeder/Cancel-Culture-Text-Analysis.

What I did

Partisan lean from a bio. Following Essig and DellaPosta (2024), 62 explicit partisan terms were split into a liberal and a conservative pole; the 1 to 9% of users with a term from one pole got a categorical lean. Every other bio term was scored by its TF-IDF cosine similarity to each pole, and each user’s continuous affiliation score is the weighted mean of their terms. That extends coverage to a third to a half of all users, at a measured cost: the continuous score agrees with the explicit labels at r = .28 to .43, an unsupervised scaling of the same bios recovers the labels at r = .13 to .44, and the partisan partition of the bio network is about as modular (Q = 0.13) as the published benchmark (0.17). Agreement with human coders is the check I have not run yet, so I quote no accuracy.

Moral content by triangulation. Each tweet was scored with the Moral Foundations Dictionary and its crowd-sourced extension, MoralBERT, Mformer, and TweetNLP’s RoBERTa emotion, offense, hate, irony, and sentiment classifiers. The outrage measure is where the dictionary’s and the transformer’s vice composites agree (r = .28 at the tweet level).

Topics. To see what people were talking about without imposing my own categories, I fit a 10-topic LDA model to the broad “cancel culture” sample (3.9 million tweets), choosing the number of topics by coherence, and compared it against BERTopic (clusters of sentence embeddings) and stLDA-C, a single-topic-per-tweet model built for short texts.

Event time. For the 57 academics, celebrities, and civilians with a datable cancellation, I aligned each target’s tweets to that target’s own peak-volume day and traced the outrage rate for 30 days on either side.

What I found

Partisanship is legible in nonpolitical self-description. People who share bio vocabulary about jobs, hobbies, faith, and family generally also share partisan lean closely enough that a usable audience segmentation comes out of profile text alone (with no third-party data). However, because the liberal bio vocabulary is more diffuse, a strict cutoff may reinforce asymmetry in the dataset. For example, in one of the subsamples, using the strictest cutoff keeps 63,500 conservative-sounding users but only 28,600 liberal-sounding ones.

The two sides bring different moral lenses to the same event. The heatmap gives the ratio of conservative-leaning to liberal-leaning authors’ per-token rate for each moral foundation in each sample. Note: this is on a log scale so a doubling and a halving are the same distance from center.

Ratio of conservative-leaning to liberal-leaning authors’ per-token rate of each moral foundation, by sample. Red means conservatives use the foundation more; blue means liberals do. Hover for the underlying rates.

Fairness showed the largest partisan gap of any foundation, but its direction depended on who was being canceled. Conservative-leaning authors used fairness language about twice as often as liberal-leaning ones when the target was an academic (2.14×) and 1.7× as often for civilians, and slightly less often in the other three samples. Cheating language leaned liberal in four of five samples. Betrayal, degradation, and the outrage proxy leaned conservative in nearly every sample, the outrage proxy by 1.07× to 1.17×. Some of this matches what Moral Foundations Theory predicts and some does not (sanctity, expected to run conservative, leaned liberal in four of five samples), which is a reason to read the heatmap one sample at a time.

The sides talk about different parts of the story. The topic model recovered themes I could name: free speech and censorship, accountability and consequences, racism and lost jobs, boycotts and party politics, and the history of the term itself, often cast as “the mob.” Conservative-leaning authors spent more of their tweets on the history-and-mob topic (13% vs. 10%) and on free speech (17% vs. 15%); liberal-leaning authors spent more on accountability and consequences (8% vs. 6%). The gaps are modest (the largest is d = 0.16). It is the same event, discussed in different vocabularies.

Anger and moral outrage are different measurements. Tweets by conservative-leaning authors scored slightly higher on the transformer’s anger (d = 0.14); tweets by liberal-leaning authors scored slightly higher on negative sentiment (d = 0.14) and irony (d = 0.11). The more useful result is a null: tweet by tweet, the anger score and the moral-outrage measure are essentially uncorrelated (r = −.05 across 8.6 million tweets). A team that picked one of them off the shelf would get a different answer than a team that picked the other.

Each target’s tweets were aligned to that target’s own peak-volume day (day 0), then pooled by type of target, weighted by volume, and smoothed over seven days. The y-axis is the rate of outrage-proxy words relative to the same group’s average in the 30 days before. Civilians are hidden by default; with six targets and few tweets per day away from the peak, their curve is mostly noise.

The burst of attention comes first; the condemnation builds behind it. For academics, outrage crested about ten days after the peak-volume day at roughly twice its pre-event rate. For celebrities it rose with the burst, peaked within the first week, and dropped back within about ten days. Averaged across targets, outrage ran higher in the month after the peak than in the month before for all three kinds of case (57 targets).

Outrage did not buy reach across the board. In the 2023 SPSP analysis (a 1.05-million-tweet subset scored with LIWC-22), anger and impoliteness predicted retweets most strongly for mid-tier “macro-influencer” accounts, more than for celebrities. In the full corpus, the outrage measure and retweet counts are essentially unrelated overall (r = .01 across 1.5 million tweets). Whatever engagement outrage earns appears to depend on who is posting it.

What this adds to interviews and surveys

People may describe a contested issue differently to a researcher than they do to each other. Interviews and surveys get the considered answer; a corpus like this one gets the unprompted one, from people who were never asked. The same scaffolding would run on open-text feedback (support tickets, portal comments, reviews): infer who is talking from how they describe themselves, compare how each group frames the same event, and watch how the tone shifts in the days after something goes wrong. Until the lean score is checked against human coders, it is best used to decide which questions an interview should ask.
