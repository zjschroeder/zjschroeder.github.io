How Liberals and Conservatives Talk About Cancel Culture

NLP
text analytics

8.6 million tweets. Liberal- and conservative-leaning users framed the same cancellations with different moral language, and outrage built in the days after attention peaked.
Published

July 22, 2026

Modified

September 23, 2026

In brief. Everyone has an opinion about cancel culture; very few of those opinions come with data. I collected 8.6 million tweets about cancellations from five samples: the phrase “cancel culture” itself, the “___ is over” hashtag trend, and tweets about specific academics, celebrities, and private citizens who were canceled. I estimated each user’s political lean from the words in their profile bio, then scored every tweet for moral and emotional language. Two findings stand out. Liberal- and conservative-leaning users discussed the same events in different moral vocabularies. And outrage did not peak when a story broke; it built over the following week or two.

The question

“Cancel culture” gets invoked with a great deal of confidence and very little data. Underneath the argument are two questions any organization that listens to the public has to answer: who is talking, and how are different groups framing the same thing?

What I did

Estimated political lean from profile bios. A small share of users (1 to 9%) state their politics outright in their bio with terms like “#BlueWave” or “GOP.” I used them as anchors, then scored every other bio word by how closely it tracks those anchors across millions of bios. That produced an estimate of lean for a third to a half of all users.

In human words: reading politics from a bio

Picture a bio that reads “dog mom, nurse, #BlueWave.” The hashtag gives away her politics. The more interesting part is that “dog mom” and “nurse” now carry a little information too, because thousands of other bios pair those words with liberal or conservative signals. Add up those small signals across a whole bio and you get a rough estimate of lean for people who never mention politics. Rough is the important word: the estimate agrees only modestly with users’ stated politics (r = .28 to .43), so it is good for comparing large groups and not for labeling any one person.

Scored moral and emotional language. I measured moral language with a published dictionary of moral words and checked it against two AI models trained to recognize the same categories. The outrage score is the rate of words about harm, cheating, betrayal, subversion, and degradation. I also ran AI classifiers for anger, sentiment, and irony.

Found topics and tracked time. A topic model sorted 3.9 million tweets into 10 themes without any categories supplied by me. For 57 people with a datable cancellation, I lined up their tweets by the day attention peaked and followed the outrage score for a month on either side.

Note

8,572,185 English-language tweets from 4,035,488 users, collected in late 2022 through Twitter’s research API. Platform terms prohibit sharing tweet or bio text, so everything here is an aggregate. Code: github.com/zjschroeder/Cancel-Culture-Text-Analysis.

What I found

Liberal and conservative users brought different moral lenses to the same events. Each cell below compares how often conservative- and liberal-leaning users used one kind of moral language in one sample.

In human words: reading the heatmap

Each number is a ratio. A 2.00 means conservative-leaning users used that kind of language twice as often as liberal-leaning users; a 0.50 means half as often. So when the person being canceled was an academic, conservatives talked about fairness about twice as much as liberals did. When it was a celebrity, they talked about purity and sanctity about half as much. The colors are scaled so that twice as much and half as much look equally strong.

Fairness showed the biggest gap, but its direction depended on who was being canceled: conservatives used it far more when the target was an academic or a private citizen, and slightly less in the other samples. Betrayal, degradation, and outrage leaned conservative in nearly every sample.

They also talked about different parts of the story. Conservative-leaning users spent more of their tweets on free speech and on the idea of “the mob”; liberal-leaning users spent more on accountability and consequences. The gaps are a few percentage points, but they point the same way across millions of tweets.

Day 0 is the day each person received the most tweets. Higher lines mean more outrage language than in the month before. Civilians are hidden by default because six cases are too few for a stable line.

In human words: lining up the timelines

Every cancellation happened on a different date, so I reset each person’s clock: day 0 is the day the most people were tweeting about them. Averaging those reset timelines shows the typical arc. For academics, the first burst of tweets carried little extra outrage; outrage roughly doubled about ten days later. For celebrities, it rose with the burst and faded within about ten days.

An anger score is not an outrage score. An off-the-shelf AI “anger” classifier and the moral-outrage measure were essentially unrelated, tweet by tweet (r = −.05). Which tool a team picks decides what it finds.

What this is good for

Interviews and surveys capture what people say when a researcher asks. A dataset like this captures what they say to each other. The same approach would work on support tickets, portal comments, or reviews: estimate who is talking, compare how each group frames the same event, and watch how the tone shifts in the days after something goes wrong.
